## 2. Homepage Structures — Patterns

Three recurring shapes:

- **Essay-first homepage** (Marginal Revolution, A Working Library, world.hey.com/dhh, Contraptions). Bio is one line at the top or a sidebar. Below it: the feed.
- **Body-of-work index** (patrickcollison.com, pmarchive.com, robinsloan.com). No hero, no CTA. A list of links by topic. Reads like a card catalog.
- **Worldview + product** (basecamp.com, pinboard.in, lynalden.com, commoncog.com). A short, declarative tagline that's a claim about the world, then product or membership.

What's missing on nearly all of them: testimonial logos, "trusted by" bars, services menus, contact-us-as-hero, video backgrounds, conversion language.

## 4. Mechanism — why refusing to sell creates demand

- **Worldview-as-filter.** "Social Bookmarking for Introverts" repels extroverts; the people left are the customer. Basecamp's "It doesn't have to be this hard" filters for people exhausted by their current tools.
- **Demonstrated thinking beats claimed expertise.** A 1,133-post archive (Ribbonfarm), a 17-year blog feed (Marginal Revolution), or 24 topic links (Patrick Collison) is harder to fake than a capabilities deck.
- **Scarcity by self-selection.** When there's no "Contact Sales" button, the inbound that arrives is pre-qualified.
- **The long-game trust signal.** Publishing for years without converting reads as confidence. Tyler Cowen has posted daily since 2003.
- **Asymmetry of taste.** Premium clients pay for taste they can recognize but couldn't produce.

## 5. The Transition Point — "yes, you can hire us"

The pattern: **the commercial offering lives one click away, in a separate room with a flat, descriptive name** ("Membership," "Premium Research," "Books"). Never on the homepage hero. Often signed by a person.

- **Cedric Chin / Commoncog**: no consulting on homepage. Membership page leads with "You are your own best investment."
- **Lyn Alden**: free newsletter → book → premium research membership.
- **Stripe Press**: never asks. The catalog is the conversion event.
- **DHH / Jason Fried personal blogs**: zero hire-me copy. Readers find the company through the writing.
- **Mandy Brown** runs a separate coaching site — A Working Library stays clean.

## 6. Anti-Patterns

- **Opacity that confuses real buyers.** A 24-link homepage with no bio works for Patrick Collison (already famous); for an unknown firm it reads cryptic.
- **Pretentious obscurity.** Vocabulary-as-status. The voice signals "I am smart" rather than "I see clearly."
- **The "I'm too cool to sell" pose.** When the absence of a CTA is performative rather than principled.
- **No path for the ready buyer.** Burying the engagement path so deep that someone holding a budget can't find it.
- **Voice as costume.** Imitating DHH's contrarianism without DHH's track record reads as posturing.

## 7. Five Takeaways for RAIN

1. **Lead with a worldview claim, not a service.** A single declarative line in the Pinboard / Basecamp / Marginal Revolution mold — a sentence that filters as much as it attracts.

2. **Make the body of work the proof.** Field notes, essays, reading, project write-ups — published consistently, dated, attributable to a person. Without a sustained writing practice, the anti-pitch posture is empty.

3. **Put the commercial offer one room away, named flatly.** A page called "Engagements" or "How to work with us" — not on the hero, linked from the nav and from individual essays. Describe what happens, in what sequence, for whom. No outcome-promises.

4. **Sign things with a human name.** Basecamp's "We'd be honored — Jason" is the warmest, most converting moment on either site, and it costs nothing.

5. **Earn the right to be quiet.** For the first 6–12 months, the homepage may need slightly more orientation than Patrick Collison's. As the body of work grows, the homepage can shed scaffolding. Build it to subtract over time.

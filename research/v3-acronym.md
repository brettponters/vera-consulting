# RAIN — Name-as-Acronym Research

## Part A — Should RAIN be an acronym?

### What the peers actually do

| Firm | Expansion on site? | Where it appears |
|---|---|---|
| IBM | No | Only historical reference to predecessor name. "International Business Machines" not on About. |
| BCG | No (effectively) | Treated as the brand. Boston/Consulting/Group surface as legal-entity references, not as a name expansion. |
| FSG | No | "FSG" only. The acronym has been formally retired into a chosen word. |
| RMI | Yes | Opening line, schema metadata, masthead. Footer drops to "RMI." |
| MIRI | Yes | Opening sentence: "The Machine Intelligence Research Institute (MIRI) is a 501(c)(3)..." |
| MITRE | Treated as a word | Stylized as Mitre/MITRE; expansion is a contested historical footnote, never on About. |
| KPMG | No | Letters refer to founder surnames; site never explains. The brand is the letters. |
| AECOM | No | Despite a clean expansion existing, the site never uses it. |
| Anthropic / DeepMind / Frog / Nava / Truss / Reos | N/A | Chosen words. No expansion machinery anywhere. |

**The pattern:** commercial consulting firms (BCG, FSG, KPMG, AECOM, IBM) collapse their letters into a brand and stop explaining. Research institutes (RMI, MIRI, ARC) keep the expansion in the opening sentence because it functions as credentialing — the full name signals what they study. Chosen-word firms (Anthropic, DeepMind, Nava) never invite the question.

### Recommendation: **HYBRID, leaning name-not-acronym.**

Treat RAIN the way Anthropic treats Anthropic and the way KPMG treats KPMG: the name is the name. Do **not** put an expansion on the homepage, the About page, or anywhere a prospective client would read it. Reserve a single optional expansion for one narrow use: the PBC charter / governing documents, where it can sit as a charter line that defines what the studio is committed to. If a regulator, an auditor, or an alignment-research grantmaker reads the charter, the expansion rewards them. Nobody else needs to see it.

### Reasoning

- **The founder's instinct is right for the surface.** "RAIN is RAIN" matches the senior-engineer voice. An expansion on the homepage immediately reads as either a values statement or a SaaS pitch — both are explicitly anti-positioned.
- **Mature consulting brands do not explain their letters.** BCG, KPMG, FSG, AECOM, IBM all earn the right to be unexplained. RAIN should aim for that posture from day one rather than starting in nonprofit-explainer mode and growing out of it.
- **The MIRI/RMI move is for nonprofits, not consulting studios.** Their expansion is the credential. RAIN's credentials are the work, the founder, and the PBC structure — not the letters.
- **An acronym on the homepage invites a category error.** Clients in finance, healthcare, and legal will read "Responsible AI [anything] Network" as marketing language and discount the rest of the site.
- **A charter-line expansion has real utility.** PBC formation documents benefit from a stated public benefit. If the letters happen to encode that benefit cleanly, the charter gets sharper at zero brand cost.
- **SEO is not a reason to expand.** "RAIN AI consulting" wins SEO via copy, schema, and backlinks — not via a name expansion buried in a footer.

### Where the expansion appears, if at all

- **Homepage:** never.
- **About page:** never.
- **Footer:** never.
- **PBC charter / Articles of Incorporation:** optional, one line, in the public-benefit clause.
- **Grant applications to alignment-research orgs:** optional, parenthetical, first mention only.
- **Press boilerplate:** never. Boilerplate reads: "RAIN is a Public Benefit Corporation providing AI consulting to regulated industries."

The test: if a candidate expansion can sit comfortably as a single line inside the PBC charter and add legal/mission specificity, it earns its keep. If it can only live on a homepage as a tagline, reject it.

### Top 3 (charter-line test)

**1. Research, Applied; Industry-Native.**
Survives the family-doctor test because every word does literal work. "Research" names the source of authority. "Applied" rejects ivory-tower posture. "Industry-native" tells a finance GC or hospital CIO that the studio knows their world. As a charter line: "RAIN — Research, Applied; Industry-Native — is a Public Benefit Corporation..." reads like a sentence a careful lawyer would sign.

**2. Research-Anchored, Industry-Native.**
Softer twin to #1. Two compound adjectives, no list. This is what you'd write if you wanted the expansion to feel less like an inventory and more like a description.

**3. Rigor, Alignment, Integrity, Notice.**
The most charter-like of the candidates and the only one that names alignment directly. "Notice" is the load-bearing choice — it carries the medical/legal sense of attentiveness and disclosure without reaching for "transparency" or "responsibility." Risk: four-noun lists can feel like a values poster. Mitigated if it appears only inside the PBC charter.

### Rejection set (constraint sketch)

- **Responsible AI Innovation Network** — every word is on the banned list. SaaS-pitch in one breath.
- **Realistic Alignment In Networked-AI** — backronym tells. "Networked-AI" is not a phrase a person says.
- **Rapid AI Integration Now** — hype-cadence; "now" is a marketing word, not a charter word.
- **Reliable Artificial Intelligence Navigator** — "navigator" is consultant-speak; "artificial intelligence" spelled out reads dated.
- **Research in AI for Nations** — overreaches. RAIN is a studio, not a policy body.

The rejection pattern: any expansion that (a) uses a banned hype word, (b) reaches for a noun-as-metaphor ("navigator," "compass," "bridge"), or (c) makes a claim larger than a small consulting studio can sign, fails the family-doctor test.

---

### Bottom line

RAIN should remain a chosen word in public — homepage, About, footer, press, all stay clean. Hold one expansion in reserve for the PBC charter where charter-line cadence is appropriate. **Top candidate for that reserved slot: "Research, Applied; Industry-Native."** It is the only one that survives being read aloud by a skeptical CFO and a skeptical alignment researcher in the same week.
